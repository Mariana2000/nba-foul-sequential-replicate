\begin{abstract}
We develop a reproducible event-level framework for testing \emph{sequential dependence} in sports officiating outcomes, applied to NBA foul calls from play-by-play data (\sampleseasons{}; 367,046 sequential observations in the main non-offensive sample). The strongest evidence concerns previous-call reversal: when the previous foul was on the home team, the next foul is about five percentage points less likely to be on the home team (sequential logit coefficient $\approx -0.28$). A within-game shuffle placebo (100 draws) eliminates this coefficient (placebo mean $\approx +0.03$; empirical $p=0/100$), supporting falsification targeted at order-defined predictors. Game- and period-level foul-count differentials show weaker and less clean associations under game fixed effects and placebo checks. Offensive fouls---analyzed separately---follow different assignment rules. We characterize results as sequential dependence in foul-call outcomes, not evidence of referee intent or referee-specific behavior.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} officiating; play-by-play; sequential dependence; falsification tests; logistic regression
